---
title: Does starting early pay off? Not once you compare like with like
date: 2026-10-26
summary: I paired Trailer Math's lead times with opening weekends for 22 sci-fi releases. Big films start early because they can afford to; they aren't big because they started early.
author: Ameer Hassan
---
I went into this study believing that starting a campaign earlier leads to a bigger opening. In this sample of 22 sci-fi releases, that belief doesn't hold up. Big-budget films start early because they can afford to; they aren't big because they started early.

## The setup

Trailer Math already records when every studio teaser and trailer lands. I paired those lead times with US opening weekends, and compared like with like:

- **One genre:** science fiction, US releases from January 2024 to October 2026. Starting in 2024 leaves out the pandemic years, when theaters were closed and many films opened on streaming the same day.
- **Three types of film:** franchise (sequels, reboots and spin-offs), adaptation (books and remakes), and original.
- **Wide releases with a reported budget:** 600 or more theaters at their widest. Seven smaller films were left out.
- **Opening weekend, not lifetime gross:** the opening is what a campaign is judged on. After it, reviews and word of mouth take over.

Openings, theater counts and budgets come from [The Numbers](https://www.the-numbers.com/) and [Saturation.io](https://saturation.io/), collected by hand.

## What I found

I split each group in half by lead time and compared the typical (median) film in each half:

- **All 22 films:** the earliest starters opened at $80.5M on a $190M budget; the latest at $4.0M on $50M.
- **Franchise (9 films):** earliest $70.5M on $205M; latest $90.6M on $203M.
- **Original (8 films):** earliest $12.5M on $98M; latest $3.4M on $18M.
- **Adaptation (5 films):** earliest $49.8M on $159M; latest $6.2M on $35M.

Across all 22 films, the early starters opened about 20 times bigger. But they also cost almost four times as much. Among franchise films, where budgets match, the early half opened *smaller*, and the latest-starting franchise film, *Spider-Man: Brand New Day*, had the biggest opening of the study.

Measured as opening weekend per dollar of budget, originals earned about the same whether they started early or late (0.17 against 0.18), and so did adaptations (0.28 against 0.30).

## Why

Budget and brand drive both things at once. A studio starts a tentpole's campaign early because it can afford to, and the film opens big because it has a budget and a fanbase behind it. Compared across all films, lead time takes the credit for both.

## What it means

For a small or mid-budget film, copying a tentpole's long runway buys nothing in this data, and since audiences forget, the smarter bet is probably to save that money for a shorter, sharper push closer to opening. That last part is my judgment, not something this study measured.

## Limits

- The groups are small, four to five films a side, so this shows no sign of an effect rather than proving there is none.
- One film moves the franchise result: *Spider-Man: Brand New Day* opened to $360M.
- *Bugonia*'s opening weekend is a 17-theater platform launch.
- Budgets are reported estimates, and *Dune: Part Two*'s 304-day lead includes the 2023 strike delay.
